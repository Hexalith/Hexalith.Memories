## What Went Well

1. Carry-forward risks became executable controls.

   Story 15.1 added release preflight behavior. Story 15.2 centralized provider/model/dimension rules. Story 15.3 added durable migration marker enforcement. Story 15.4 enforced token endpoint transport policy. These are not just notes; they are code, tests, and operator guidance.

2. Reviews found real issues and the team followed through.

   The story records show adversarial review patches across release tooling, registry behavior, migration marker consistency, transport sanitization, and AppHost boot orchestration. Most critical findings were patched before story closure.

3. Deferred-work semantics improved.

   Story 15.5 separated resolved entries, accepted risks, and carried-forward candidates. It proposed three bounded follow-up stories instead of turning the entire historical register into backlog.

4. Runtime validation became more credible.

   Story 15.6 did real AppHost and `/ready` validation, including Docker/Aspire resource checks and Redis failure behavior. This improved confidence in local orchestration rather than relying only on source-text checks.

5. Operator guidance kept pace with hardening.

   `docs/operations/embedding-providers.md`, release runbook guidance, DAPR component template comments, and story artifacts now explain the operational contracts created by the epic.

## Challenges

1. Scope discipline repeatedly needed repair.

   Stories 15.2, 15.3, 15.4, and 15.5 record submodule pointer bumps or planning-artifact changes as accepted scope deviations. Story 15.6 records additional file-scope overrides. The repo improved, but the implementation history became harder to review cleanly.

2. Some accepted risks remain intentional.

   Examples include `12.1-RV3` skip-CI semantics, `13.1-RV10` and `13.3-RV8` casing/persistence semantics, and `13.6-RV3` migration result-surface shape. These are documented decisions, not forgotten work.

3. Hardening work exposed old foundational assumptions.

   Story 15.6 had to reach back into Story 1.1 scaffolding behavior, dynamic ports, DAPR component generation, submodule detection, and health checks. The lesson is that foundational review findings can stay relevant long after the original story is marked done.

4. Follow-up risk volume remains non-zero.

   Epic 15 closed high-value risks, but new review carry-forwards were recorded, especially around release preflight edge cases, migration marker operator safety, deferred schema cleanliness, and AppHost lifecycle hardening.

5. No Epic 16 plan exists yet.

   `epics.md` does not define Epic 16. The next step needs a directional planning decision rather than automatic continuation.

## Significant Discoveries

1. Operational hardening is product work.

   Release preflight, provider validation, migration coordination, token transport policy, and readiness checks directly affect whether operators can trust the system.

2. Scope overrides are useful, but should be rare.

   Scope-Override blocks preserved honesty, but the recurrence shows a process smell. Future stories should isolate submodule pointer bumps and unrelated planning edits into dedicated commits.

3. Write-boundary protection matters more than early checks alone.

   Story 15.3 showed that generation-time checks are insufficient for stale workflows. The final semantic write boundary must enforce the migration marker target.

4. Local-development exceptions must be literal and test-pinned.

   Story 15.4 kept loopback HTTP usable for local and fake-server cases while rejecting production-risky non-loopback HTTP endpoints.

5. The next epic should be chosen, not inferred.

   Story 15.5 proposes three follow-up stories, but `epics.md` has no Epic 16. The next planning step should decide whether to continue operational hardening, convert the proposals into backlog, or pivot to a Phase 2 feature track.
